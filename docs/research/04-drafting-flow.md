# 04 — Drafting flow, distraction and attention, across many kinds of writers

Research notes for Quarry's drafting experience: the CodeMirror/Markdown prose editor (focus mode, typewriter scrolling, autosave, snapshots), the phone capture inbox, and the rule-based side panel. Quarry does not generate or suggest prose, so this file is about the conditions around the writer: what to show, what to hide, when to speak up, and how to get ideas from a phone back into a draft.

**How to read the evidence labels**

- **[R]** Empirical research: a peer-reviewed study, experiment or systematic field study. The sample and its limits are noted.
- **[R-weak]** Research with serious limits: small N, never replicated, or known only through secondary summaries.
- **[P]** Practitioner or anecdotal: craft books, author interviews, product docs, vendor claims. These are useful for design, but they are not evidence.

The craft literature on drafting is far larger than the research on it. Most "rules" writers follow (stop mid-sentence, morning pages, daily word counts) have little direct experimental support. Where research exists, it mostly backs the general mechanisms behind them: interruptions are costly, judging while producing slows production, small visible progress motivates, and suggestions steer content.

## 1. Flow: what it needs and what breaks it

- **[P/R-theory] Csikszentmihalyi's flow conditions.** Clear goals at each step, immediate feedback, a balance between challenge and skill, distractions kept out of mind, no worry about failure, self-consciousness fading, and an activity rewarding in itself. Bernoff applies this to writers. He argues that phones and social feeds break several conditions at once: they split attention, and they bring back self-consciousness and fear of judgement (likes, replies). His advice is to prepare research and a "fat outline" beforehand and then protect an uninterrupted block. Source: Josh Bernoff, https://bernoff.com/blog/mihaly-csikszentmihalyi-showed-us-flow-writers-need-flow-and-tech-destroys-it (summarising Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*, 1990). This is a blog summary of the theory. The design reading is still useful: the writer needs **clear goals and feedback**, but feedback that brings in **evaluation and self-consciousness** works against flow.
- **[R] Progress as the strongest daily motivator.** Amabile & Kramer's diary study (238 knowledge workers, 7 companies, about 12,000 daily diary entries) found that *making progress in meaningful work* was the most common feature of people's best days. Small wins counted nearly as much as big ones. Setbacks weighed two to three times more than progress. Source: *The Progress Principle* (2011), https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html. The data are correlational diaries, not experiments. For an editor this suggests that visible, low-key progress signals (words today, scene done) can feed flow. Signals that frame the session as a failure ("target missed") cost more than a success signal earns.

## 2. Interruptions and getting back into the work

- **[R] Interrupted people work faster but pay in stress.** Mark, Gudith & Klocke (CHI 2008) ran 48 participants through an email-writing task with interruptions every 2 minutes. Interrupted participants finished *faster*, with no loss of quality. They wrote **shorter** messages, and after only about 20 minutes they reported significantly higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Whether the interruption was on the same topic or a different one made no difference: *any* interruption changed the way people worked. Source: https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf. For prose this matters, because interruptions push writers toward shorter, more hurried text.
- **[R] Fragmented work in the field.** Mark's field observation of 36 information workers found people spent about 3 minutes on an event before switching. 81.9% of interrupted work was resumed the same day, after about 23 minutes on average, with roughly two other tasks in between. About 44% of interruptions were **self-initiated**. Source: Gallup interview, https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx (fieldwork published as "No Task Left Behind?", CHI 2005). The often-quoted "23 minutes" is time to *return* to the task, not time to regain focus. It is one study.
- **[R] Attention spans on screens are shrinking, and self-interruption is a big share.** In *Attention Span* (2023), Mark reports that average attention on any one screen fell from about 2.5 minutes (2004) to about 47 seconds in recent measurements, and that people interrupt themselves roughly as often as others interrupt them. Sources: https://www.deseret.com/2023/1/25/23569188/attention-span-is-shrinking/ and https://www.informatics.uci.edu/?p=7800. These measure general computer work, not drafting. The design point is that the threat is often **the writer's own urge to check something**. An editor that makes "checking" one click away (stats, research, the inbox) invites the switch.
- **[R] Attention residue.** Leroy (2009, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2)) found that after switching tasks, part of a person's attention stays on the unfinished previous task, which lowers performance on the new one. Later work with Glomb proposes a **"Ready-to-Resume" plan**: before switching, jot down where you are and what comes next. This reduces residue. Sources: https://www.uwb.edu/news/2024/05/17/an-ongoing-study-on-the-success-of-staying-focused, https://strategy-business.com/article/re00088. For Quarry, an idea pulled in mid-draft is itself an interruption, and a one-line "where I am / next" note is a tested way to soften both stopping and resuming.
- **[R] Defer notifications to breakpoints.** Iqbal & Bailey (CHI 2007/2008) built models that detect natural breakpoints in free-form tasks (69–87% accuracy depending on breakpoint type). Holding notifications until a breakpoint *reduced frustration and reaction time* compared with delivering them immediately. Sources: https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal_Bailey-CHI07.pdf, https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI08.pdf, survey https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171. In a prose editor, breakpoints are easy to spot: end of paragraph, end of scene, pause longer than N seconds, session end.
- **[R] A notification you don't act on still costs you.** Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015, *JEP: Human Perception and Performance* 41(4)) found that merely *receiving* a phone notification, without answering it, significantly disrupted an attention-demanding task. The effect was comparable to actually using the phone, probably because it prompts mind-wandering. Source: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/motr/youre-distracted-even-when-you-ignore-that-text.html. A badge, toast or panel that lights up mid-sentence is not free, even if the writer ignores it.

## 3. The cognition of drafting: producing vs judging, and blocks

- **[R] Planning, translating and reviewing compete for working memory.** Kellogg's model, supported in a 2013 progress report, finds that planning ideas, turning them into sentences and reviewing text all draw on the same limited central executive. Sources: https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/690, https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/304. This is the mechanism behind "don't edit while you draft": every review prompt the interface raises takes capacity from translation.
- **[R] Fluent writing comes in bursts between pauses.** Keystroke-logging research (Kaufer, Hayes & Flower 1986; Chenoweth & Hayes 2001, *Written Communication* 18) treats the length of text produced between pauses of 2 seconds or more as a core fluency measure. More skilled writers produce longer bursts. Source (secondary): https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/1318/979. Pauses are *where planning happens*. An interface that reacts to every pause (a pop-up after 2 seconds of idle) intrudes on the writer's thinking time.
- **[R-weak] Writer's block comes from rigid rules, not anxiety.** Mike Rose (1980, *College Composition and Communication* 31.4) compared 5 blocked and 5 fluent UCLA student writers. Blocked writers worked from rigid, algorithm-like rules ("the introduction must grab the reader first", "never repeat a word") and inflexible plans. Fluent writers used loose heuristics they could drop. Rose's line, as quoted in summaries: writers with "the least precise rules and plans have the least trouble composing." Sources: https://www.mikerosebooks.com/writer-s-block--the-cognitive-dimension.html, https://ijnet.org/en/story/tips-overcoming-writers-block. This is small and qualitative but influential, and it bears directly on a *rule-based* side panel. Rules shown as absolute verdicts during drafting are exactly the rigid rules Rose found in blocked writers.

## 4. Session rhythm: brief daily sessions, binges, sprints, breaks, stopping points

- **[R-weak] Boice: brief daily sessions beat binges.** Boice's studies of new faculty reported that scheduled brief daily writing (about 30 minutes) produced far more pages and more "creative ideas" than waiting for inspiration or saving writing for big blocks. Binge writers in his 1997 study wrote less and scored higher on depression measures. Sources: https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1997-boice.pdf, Krashen's reanalysis https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf.
- **[R] …but daily writing is not what successful writers actually do.** Helen Sword (2016, *Int. J. for Academic Development* 21(4)) reread Boice. The key studies had 9–10 subjects per group, all self-identified procrastinators. They were intensively monitored, were never replicated, and dropout was high: 15 of 42 left, most citing the *nuisance of charting*. Sword then surveyed 1,223 academics and interviewed 100 peer-nominated successful writers in 15 countries. Only about 12% write every weekday, and only 2 of the 100 followed a Boice-like regime. Some prolific writers binge, some need weeks of unbroken time, and some write with email on. What successful writers share is a mix of discipline, craft pride, social support and positive emotion (her "BASE"), not one schedule. She warns against advice that loads guilt onto writers who don't comply. Source: https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/Write-every-day-a-mantra-dismantled.pdf. This is the strongest evidence in the set for **making targets, streaks and schedules optional and guilt-free**.
- **[R, mixed] Fixed vs self-chosen breaks.** Biwer et al. (2023, *British Journal of Educational Psychology*, N=87 students in real self-study) found fixed breaks (24/6 or 12/3 minutes) gave less fatigue and more concentration and motivation than self-chosen breaks, with the same amount done. A 2025 study comparing Pomodoro with self-paced "Flowtime" found no overall difference in fatigue or motivation. Sources: https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel, https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/7/861. Timed sprints help some people. A hard stop that cuts into a burst conflicts with flow, so a timer should **end softly** (finish the sentence or paragraph first).
- **[R] The "Hemingway effect".** Oyama, Manalo & Nakatani (2018, *Thinking Skills and Creativity* 30) interrupted 260 undergraduates during a copying task. Those who were *closer to finishing* reported more motivation to resume than those further away or those who had finished. In a second study (N=131) the effect appeared only for **structured** tasks, where people could judge what remained. Source: https://researchmap.jp/read0151030/published_papers/22619952; summary https://bigthink.com/the-learning-curve/the-hemingway-effect/. This is lab evidence that stopping with a clear, near "next step" helps restarting. It works when the writer knows what comes next.
- **[P] Hemingway's original advice.** In "Monologue to the Maestro" (*Esquire*, October 1935) he advised stopping while it is going well and you know what will happen next. He also described rereading the work from the start each day, correcting as he went, before continuing. When the book got long, he reread the last two or three chapters daily and all of it weekly. Source: https://fs.blog/ernest-hemingway-three-tips-on-how-to-write-fiction/. Stopping *mid-sentence* is a later embellishment, not Hemingway's words (https://www.writingforums.com/threads/hemingways-curious-trick-stop-while-youre-going-good.139596/). The reread-to-warm-up habit is an important nuance: many writers *do* a little light editing as the on-ramp to a session.

## 5. Drafting practices from the craft tradition [P]

- **Peter Elbow, freewriting (1973).** Write for 10 minutes and never stop. If you can't find a word or spelling, put in a squiggle and keep going. Freewriting should get *no feedback at all*. Editing itself is fine. The problem is editing *at the same time* as producing, which he says both blocks writing and flattens voice. Source: excerpt from *Writing Without Teachers*, https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/FREEWRITING-by-Peter-Elbow.pdf. The squiggle is the original placeholder convention, a forerunner of journalists' "TK".
- **Anne Lamott, "Shitty First Drafts" (*Bird by Bird*, 1994).** Even very good writers produce bad first drafts, and permission to write badly is what gets the draft written. The title anecdote ("bird by bird") is about breaking an overwhelming task into small units. Critics note that the book says little about revision. Sources: https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/anne-lamott/bird-by-bird, https://newrepublic.com/article/203899/anne-lamott-battle-writer-block.
- **Julia Cameron, Morning Pages (*The Artist's Way*, 1992).** Three pages of longhand, stream-of-consciousness writing first thing each morning, never reread at the time and not meant as "writing". Source: https://hayhouse.co.uk/morning-pages-journal.
- **Dorothea Brande, *Becoming a Writer* (1934).** Get up early and, before talking or reading, write fast and without judgement. The aim is to catch the half-awake mind before it starts imitating whatever it reads. She also advocates writing at a set time to train the habit. Sources: https://www.supersummary.com/becoming-a-writer/summary/, https://writersalliance.org/becoming-a-writer/.
- **Stephen King, *On Writing* (2000).** "Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open" (advice he credits to editor John Gould). The first draft is private, and only revision lets readers in. Source: https://www.shortform.com/blog/revision-in-writing/.
- **NaNoWriMo / "write first, edit later".** 50,000 words in November, about 1,667 a day, started by Chris Baty in 1999. Speed and a public count were used to silence the inner editor. Sources: https://writerunboxed.com/2007/10/19/interview-nanos-chris-baty-part-1/, https://mebondbooks.com/2016/11/14/history-nanowrimo. The nonprofit **shut down in April 2025** (money problems, falling participation, controversy over its AI stance), and the event was relaunched elsewhere as "Novel November". Sources: https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20250407/97466-nanowrimo-nonprofit-shutters.html, https://storyempire.com/2025/04/28/nanowrimo-closing-what-went-wrong-what-will-replace/. The *practice* of word sprints lives on in writing communities. The AI controversy is worth noting given Quarry's no-AI stance.
- **Paul Graham (essays/blogging).** Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can, then rewrite it again and again, cutting hard. Read it aloud to find where you stumble. Expect most of an essay's ideas to arrive *after* you start writing. Source: "Writing, Briefly", https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html.
- **Write or Die (Dr Wicked).** An app that punishes stopping: after a grace period, an unpleasant sound plays, or in "kamikaze" mode your text starts deleting itself. Sources: https://fictionwritersreview.com/shoptalk/writeor-die/, https://v2.writeordie.com. It shows that some writers *want* coercive momentum tools. It is also the opposite of Sword's and Amabile's findings on guilt and setbacks. This belongs in an opt-in mode, never in the default.
- **Freewrite (Astrohaus) hardware.** A writing device deliberately built with very limited editing: you can add text but barely revise it. Drafts sync to the cloud to be edited elsewhere. Users praise the momentum, and critics find the missing editing frustrating. Sources: https://getfreewrite.com/pages/faq, https://www.ianbetteridge.com/writing-with-the-freewrite/. The market supports "drafting mode" and "editing mode" as distinct states.

**Cross-cutting pattern.** Every kind of writer keeps **intent and plan** in view (beat card, lede, outline note, the next step), and sometimes **source material** too. They push **evaluation** (spelling, formatting, references, line polish, readers) out of the drafting moment. Technical writers are the exception that proves it: they accept continuous rule checks because consistency, not voice, is the goal. Even they surface those checks in a separate channel (problems pane, CI) rather than as a stream of interruptions.

## 7. Capturing ideas on the go, and getting them back into the work

- **[R] Information scraps.** Bernstein, Van Kleek, Karger & schraefel (2008, *ACM TOIS* 26(4)) studied 27 knowledge workers. People keep short notes outside their "proper" tools (Post-its, emails to self, loose text files) because those places offer **fast capture, unconstrained content and organisation, and availability when needed**. Unmet needs they list for tools: lightweight entry, unconstrained content, flexible use, **visibility**, and **mobility**. Sources: https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/266461, https://people.csail.mit.edu/msbernst/papers/TOIS-final-with-refs.pdf. A follow-up longitudinal study (List-it, CHI 2011) found four distinct keeping strategies and uses ranging from reminders to brainstorming to journaling. Source: https://people.csail.mit.edu/emax/papers/chi2011-finders-keepers.pdf. For Quarry's inbox: capture must be **faster than a text to yourself**, accept anything (half a sentence, a voice clip, a photo of a page), and avoid asking for structure at capture time. Classification belongs later.
- **[P] Joan Didion, "On Keeping a Notebook" (1968).** Her notebook was never an accurate record of facts. It kept *how things felt* and what caught her attention: fragments too short to use yet, kept so she could reconnect with earlier selves. Sources: https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/11/19/joan-didion-on-keeping-a-notebook/, https://fs.blog/keeping-a-notebook/. Many captured sparks are moods, images and overheard lines, not plot notes, and they may never map neatly onto a scene.
- **[P] Voice and phone capture.** Writing guides recommend the built-in voice recorder or notes app for ideas in transit. One reviewer's week of capturing only by voice found it good for sparks and poor for deadline work. Vendor products (Plaud, Voicenotes) market capture-while-walking to writers. Sources: https://www.thebookdesigner.com/10-ways-free-iphone-apps-supercharge-writers/, https://tech.yahoo.com/apps/articles/voice-notes-replace-writing-idea-110710093.html. No systematic survey of novelists' capture habits turned up. This is a gap.
- **[R] Getting ideas back in.** The research on resumption and scraps points the same way. Captured ideas are valuable *when they resurface at the right moment* (visibility), but surfacing them in the middle of a burst is an interruption. The Iqbal & Bailey breakpoint result suggests showing the inbox at session start, at scene breaks or on demand, not as live pings while drafting.

## 8. Interface research on writing tools

- **[R] Predictive text makes writing shorter and more predictable.** Arnold, Chauncey & Gajos (IUI 2020) had people write image captions with suggestions hidden, always shown (three next words, as on phone keyboards), or shown only when the system was confident. With suggestions, captions were **shorter, used fewer unpredicted words and fewer adjectives** (e.g. colours dropped when a noun was suggested right after "a"). The authors call this "skip nudging". Phrase suggestions made writing even more predictable than word suggestions. Positively biased suggestions made people write more positively. Sources: https://seas.harvard.edu/news/predictive-text-systems-change-what-we-write, https://hci.seas.harvard.edu/publications/predictive-text-encourages-predictable-writing, https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/37365774/ARNOLD-DISSERTATION-2020.pdf. This supports Quarry's no-suggestion stance. It also applies to *rule-based* panels: any visible suggestion of a word, length or phrasing nudges content.
- **[R] More parallel suggestions help ideation but cost efficiency.** Buschek, Zürn & Eiband (CHI 2021, N=156 email writers): showing 0/1/3/6 phrase suggestions, more options helped ideation but slowed writing. Non-native speakers accepted more suggestions. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09157. Evaluating options mid-sentence is review work.
- **[R-weak] Side-margin feedback can support revision without generating text.** Dang, Benharrak, Lehmann & Buschek (UIST 2022) showed paragraph summaries in the margin, updated continuously. Writers (N=4 and N=8) used them as an outside view to check scope and revise paragraphs. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09323. This is evidence that **margin/side information is used as a reflection tool during revision**. The study was too small to say whether live updating disrupts drafting.
- **[R] Design space for writing assistants.** Lee et al. (CHI 2024) reviewed 115 papers and treat *interaction* (where, when and how the tool's output appears, and who starts it) as a core design dimension alongside task, user, technology and ecosystem. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14117. This is useful vocabulary for specifying Quarry's panel: who initiates (writer vs system), when (continuous vs on demand), and where (inline vs separate).
- **[P] Spell-check squiggles.** No controlled study turned up. Practitioner and institutional guidance converges, though: live squiggles pull writers into fixing mid-sentence. Microsoft's own support page for Word on the web suggests turning live checking off while drafting and checking the whole document afterwards. Teachers report students stopping mid-sentence to clear red lines. Sources: https://support.microsoft.com/en-au/office/turn-spell-check-on-or-off-in-word-for-the-web-fe0b5644-10e6-4e61-b661-441bff362a84, https://katemessner.com/turn-off-the-spell-check/, https://libguides.royalroads.ca/ugrad_essay/write. This fits Kellogg's working-memory competition and Elbow's "editing at the same time".
- **[P] Focus and typewriter modes.** iA Writer's focus mode dims everything except the current sentence or paragraph. Typewriter mode keeps the cursor line vertically centred. The company recommends **turning focus mode off for editing** and notes that centring can make the screen jump when selecting text. Sources: https://ia.net/writer/support/editor/focus-mode, https://www.thebookdesigner.com/writer-for-ipad-by-oliver-reichenstein-information-architects/. Focus mode is a *drafting* state, and its known friction is with selection and editing.
- **[P] Constraint devices and modes.** Freewrite (no editing), Write or Die (punishment) and Scrivener's composition/full-screen mode all show writers choosing **explicit mode switches** between generating and revising. See sections 5–6.

## Implications for Quarry's drafting experience

### A. Two explicit states: Drafting and Revising
1. **Make "Drafting" a named mode.** Focus mode, typewriter scrolling and the quiet panel are on together. "Revising" turns on review aids. Writers across genres separate these (Elbow, King, Graham, Freewrite, iA's own guidance), and Kellogg's model explains why. One keystroke toggles it, and the mode is remembered per document.
2. **Typewriter scrolling should let go during selection and mouse editing.** This avoids the screen-jump iA warns about. Re-centre only on the next typed character.
3. **Allow light "warm-up editing".** Hemingway reread and corrected before continuing. Opening a session should show the last paragraph or two in full contrast, and editing them should feel normal. Only the *system's* evaluation stays quiet, not the writer's.

### B. What is visible while drafting vs only on demand
4. **Always visible, and minimal:** the text; the current paragraph highlighted (if focus is on); a small, non-judgemental session indicator (words this session, or elapsed time; the writer picks one, or none); and a save-state dot that never animates or pops. Autosave and snapshots stay silent. Show only a failure, and show it plainly.
5. **Optionally pinned "intent" card.** One short note for the current scene: the beat, its goal, or the Hemingway-style "what happens next" left last session. This is the screenwriter's index card and the journalist's lede, and it serves flow's "clear goals" condition. It is the one piece of planning that earns a place on screen.
6. **On demand only (one key or click, never pushed):** the side panel's rule findings, the capture inbox, document and chapter stats, the outline, research notes, snapshot history, and spelling. Live spell squiggles are **off by default in Drafting** and on in Revising, with a per-user override. Some writers (and non-native writers) will want them.
7. **No inline marks from rules while drafting.** No underlines, gutter icons or counters that change as you type. If a rule *must* indicate something in Drafting mode, use one quiet aggregate cue (a dim dot on the panel handle) that does not change while the writer is mid-burst.

### C. How and when the rule-based panel speaks
8. **Write rules as heuristics, not verdicts** (Rose). Use wording like "Possibly repetitive: 'suddenly' ×4 in this scene", never "Error". Every rule can be dismissed per instance, muted per document, and switched off globally. Nothing about a rule should suggest the writer is failing a standard.
9. **Show findings at breakpoints, not on idle** (Iqbal & Bailey; Chenoweth & Hayes on pauses as planning time). Valid triggers: the writer opens the panel; the session ends; a scene or chapter break is typed; the mode switches to Revising. Never trigger on a 2-second typing pause. Even an ignored notification costs attention (Stothart).
10. **Don't suggest replacement text, even from rules.** Suggestions shorten text, make it more predictable, and nudge content (Arnold/Gajos; Buschek). Rules can *point at* things such as counts, repetitions, a POV-character mismatch or a continuity conflict with notes. They should not offer "use X instead". This keeps the panel consistent with Quarry's no-AI, no-suggestion principle.
11. **Use the panel for reflection in Revising.** An outside view (per-paragraph structure, scene word counts, which inbox ideas link to this scene) is what Dang et al. found writers used. Keep it static while the writer types, and refresh on pause or on request.

### D. Interruptions and ideas mid-draft
12. **Quick-stash without leaving the line.** One shortcut opens a tiny inline field (or lets the writer select a sentence and stash it). Typing a thought and pressing Enter sends it to the inbox, tagged with the current document and position, and the cursor returns where it was. This turns the self-interruption Mark describes into a 3-second detour, and acts like a mini Ready-to-Resume note (Leroy).
13. **First-class placeholders.** A TK-style marker (e.g. `TK`, `[[?]]` or Elbow's squiggle) for a missing name, fact or word. It is never flagged while drafting, but is gathered into a "To come" list in the panel in Revising. Journalists use this to avoid stopping for research, and it covers academics' missing citations and novelists' unnamed minor characters.
14. **The phone inbox never pushes into a drafting session.** No toasts or badges that change mid-session. New captures show as a count only when the writer opens the inbox, at session start, or at session end ("3 new ideas since last session"). That is the breakpoint principle applied to capture.
15. **Resume notes at session end.** When the writer ends a session (or closes the window after more than N minutes), optionally prompt once: "Where are you / what happens next?" (one line, skippable). Show it pinned at the top on return. This combines Hemingway's "know what happens next", the lab Hemingway effect (which works for *structured* next steps), and Leroy's resume plan. Also restore the exact cursor and scroll position.

### E. Phone capture inbox
16. **Capture faster than a text to yourself** (Bernstein et al.): open straight to an input, accept text, voice and a photo, require no project, tags or title, and work offline. Don't transcribe or summarise with AI. Store voice as audio, with on-device dictation only if the writer asks for it, in line with the no-AI stance.
17. **Triage is a separate, later moment.** At the desk, the inbox offers "attach to scene/chapter", "send to notes", "keep loose" and "archive". Accept that many captures are Didion-style fragments of mood or image that belong in a loose "commonplace" area, not on a scene.
18. **Bring ideas back at the right moment.** When a scene is opened, show (on demand) the captures attached to it. In Revising, list unattached captures in the panel so they surface without interrupting a burst.

### F. Sessions, targets and sprints
19. **Targets are optional, and so is the writer's style.** Support daily targets, project targets, sprint timers and no targets at all, with **no default streak**. Sword's data show that successful writers vary widely and that guilt-driven regimes cause dropout. Boice's own subjects quit over the bother of charting.
20. **Frame progress, never failure** (Amabile & Kramer). Show what was done ("612 words · scene 14 drafted"), not what was missed. No red, no broken-streak messages. A missed target simply isn't mentioned.
21. **Sprints end softly.** A timer (25/5 and custom) that, when time runs out, waits for the end of the sentence or paragraph, or for a pause, before showing a small "time's up". It never locks the editor (Biwer et al.; flow). Offer a Write-or-Die-style "keep going" nudge only as an explicit opt-in.
22. **Optional session ritual, for those who want it.** A "pages" space (Cameron/Brande/Elbow) that is unjudged and unlinted, excluded from project counts, and private by default. It is a warm-up area, separate from the manuscript.

### G. Respecting different kinds of writers
23. **Kind-of-writing presets, not one ideal.** "Novel" (quiet drafting, beats card), "Screenplay" (Fountain-aware highlighting, beat card pinned), "Non-fiction/academic" (split view with sources or notes, citation keys left unresolved while drafting), "Poetry" (no typewriter recentring, generous line spacing, a reading-aloud-friendly view, no word targets by default), "Docs" (rules allowed live in the panel, since consistency is the goal). Each preset only changes defaults, so every option stays reachable.
24. **Notes-first starts.** For writers who begin from notes (Zettelkasten, journalists' notebooks), allow a new scene or chapter to start from selected inbox items or notes placed as a scaffold above the cursor. The writer then writes over or between them. No blank page, and nothing generated.
