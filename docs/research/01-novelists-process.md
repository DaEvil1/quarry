# How novelists actually write and revise

Research for **Quarry**, a bookkeeping-only novel-writing app. The target user is a pantser who writes long fantasy chapter by chapter, keeps a large series bible, rewrites chapters by salvaging pieces of old ones, and gets ideas from reading and conversations. Quarry never generates or suggests story content.

This file collects concrete, attributed habits from about 30 authors and sources. Each one covers how drafts get written, how continuity is tracked, how revision happens, what happens when an idea arrives mid-draft, and which tools people use and why.

**How reliable the sources are.** Each source is tagged:
- **[P]** Primary: the author's own words, from their blog, FAQ, a Q&A they answered, or a published lecture or essay.
- **[S]** Secondary: journalism or a reputable write-up of an interview.
- **[T]** Tertiary: blog summaries, fan wikis, "writing routine" aggregators or forum relays. Treat these as anecdotal.

Several famous claims could not be checked against a primary text and are flagged where they appear. These include King's *On Writing* wording, Atwood's "piles on the floor" and Lee Child's revision habits. No source was copied at length. Each source has at most one short quote.

## 1. Plotters, pantsers and everyone in between

- **George R. R. Martin (gardener).** Martin offers the architect/gardener split. Architects plan the whole house; gardeners plant a seed and see what grows. He calls himself mostly a gardener and says most writers mix the two. He knows the ending and the fates of the main characters, but finds the route while writing. [S] Wheeler Centre 2013, https://www.wheelercentre.com/news-stories/2013/architects-gardeners-and-game-of-thrones ; NPR interview summary, https://www.npr.org/2019/10/19/771377681/george-r-r-martin-really-does-know-you-want-him-to-write-faster
- **Stephen King (situation, not plot).** King starts from a "what if" situation and digs out the story, which he treats as a fossil that already exists. He distrusts plotting as incompatible with spontaneity. [T] Secondary summaries of *On Writing*, e.g. https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/14896944-a-writer-s-question-to-plot-or-not-to-plot
- **Lee Child (no outline, linear).** Child starts on page one with no plan and makes up each Reacher book line by line. Louise Ure, a fellow author, reports that he rereads and edits the previous day's work before going on. She also says he sends the manuscript off on finishing the last scene without rereading the whole book. Her account is secondhand, and other summaries contradict the "no whole-book revision" part. [S] Newstalk ZB, https://www.newstalkzb.co.nz/on-air/mike-hosking-breakfast/audio/lee-child-i-have-no-clue-no-plan-no-outline/ ; [T] Murderati, https://murderati.com/oh-to-be-lee-child/
- **Terry Pratchett ("draft zero").** Pratchett said he did not plan books in advance. He could see "bits of the story", a rough start and the ending. His private "draft zero" was him telling himself the story: written on auto-pilot without editing, then edited hard the next day. Nobody saw draft zero. [S] Longreads / Internet Writing Journal 2000, https://longreads.com/?p=15238
- **Ursula K. Le Guin (varies per book).** Le Guin usually knew roughly where a story began and ended, and was often wrong. She discovered characters by writing them and could not start until she knew a character's name. One novel was fully rewritten five times over 20+ years; another was drafted in six weeks with only word-level changes. Her view: "The book is done when you say it's done." [P] Hachette reader Q&A, https://www.hachette.co.uk/ursula-k-le-guin-2/2015/10/18/ursula-le-guin-answers-your-questions/
- **Zadie Smith (Micro Manager vs Macro Planner).** Smith offers a second useful split. Macro Planners plan up front, rearrange and may write from the middle. Micro Managers like her go line by line, perfecting each section before moving on, and do not know the ending until they reach it. She obsesses over the first ~20 pages until the voice is right, after which the rest comes faster. She edits as she goes and keeps essentially one draft. [P] "That Crafty Feeling", The Believer, https://thebeliever.net/?p=40223
- **Brandon Sanderson (hybrid).** Sanderson also uses Martin's terms and says nobody is a pure type. He describes discovering his characters by writing them and outlining the plot once he knows who they are. His lectures open with the principle that there is no one way to write a story. [S] Reactor on his BYU lectures, https://reactormag.com/brandon-sanderson-video-lectures-writing-sci-fi-fantasy/
- **N.K. Jemisin (loose outline, often abandoned).** Jemisin outlines "vague ideas", sometimes forgets the outline, improvises for a while, then steers back. She writes "test chapters" to find a voice. Her second-person chapter in *The Fifth Season* began as one written on a whim. [P] Electric Literature, https://electricliterature.com/they-are-living-their-own-myths-an-interview-with-n-k-jemisin-author-of-the-fifth-season/
- **Joe Abercrombie (plans close, then loose).** Abercrombie plans the first part of a book chapter by chapter. Later parts get only a setting, a rough plot and what each POV character must do. He treats the plan as something that bends: plot and character change to fit each other during drafting, and he left the *Red Country* ending open until the last part. [P] Gollancz blog "On Planning", https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/16/joe-abercrombie-on-planning/
- **James Patterson (heavy outliner).** Patterson writes outlines of roughly 60–80 pages, about a paragraph per chapter. His co-writers draft from them, and he then runs one to five revision drafts. [S] Gulf News, https://gulfnews.com/general/james-patterson-a-factory-of-hand-tooled-novels-1.1149083
- **Dean Wesley Smith ("writing into the dark").** Smith is the best-known advocate of no-outline, minimal-rewrite drafting. His "cycling" method makes small edits and fixes while moving forward, instead of a big second draft. Nearly all sources on this are by Smith or his followers. [T] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Wesley_Smith ; [P] book listing, https://wmgpublishing.gumroad.com/l/KhsBW

**What this means.** "Pantser" is not one process. Pantsers differ on whether they edit as they go: Smith and Smith (Zadie) do, King and Roberts don't. They differ on whether the ending is known: Martin knows it, Child doesn't. And they differ on whether they draft in order: Child and Hobb do, Gabaldon doesn't. Several authors stress that their process changes from book to book (Le Guin, Abercrombie, Jemisin).

## 2. Drafting: forward motion, "don't revise while drafting", re-entry

- **King: door closed, then door open.** The first draft is written alone, "with the door closed", with no outside input. Revision is done "with the door open". [T] https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2088345-2nd-draft-1st-draft---10
- **Nora Roberts: no going back.** Her first draft is a fast "discovery draft" written without returning to fiddle with earlier pages. She trusts that problems can be fixed later, and calls this the hardest stage. [S] Writers Write, https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1252769
- **Anne Lamott: three drafts.** Lamott passes on a friend's three-draft framing. The "down draft" just gets it down, the "up draft" fixes it up, and the "dental draft" checks every tooth. Her famous point is that good writers start with bad first drafts. [P] Chapter excerpt, https://www.brandeis.edu/writing-program/shitty-first-drafts.pdf
- **Abercrombie: from editing as he went to drafting straight through.** Early on, Abercrombie polished every sentence and night's work before moving on, which helped him find his voice. Later he drafted straight through, gave each finished *part* a light tidy, sent it to his editor and family, and then left it alone. Big revision was deferred to one whole-manuscript pass, which he found "a good deal more efficient". His aim is about 1,500 words a day, with wide variation. [P] "On Writing", https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/17/joe-abercrombie-on-writing/ ; "On Revising", https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/18/joe-abercrombie-on-revising/
- **Diana Gabaldon: no separate revision.** Gabaldon spends 2–5 days per scene, going over it repeatedly. She says she does not write and then revise; the two are one continuous process. A finished scene is as good as she can make it at that point. Her quota is about 1,000 words a day. [P] Writer's Digest, https://www.writersdigest.com/january-12/get-more-gabaldon
- **Sanderson: revises each section right after drafting it.** For *Oathbringer*, the "rough draft" he turned in was effectively a 3.0, because he had revised each section right after writing it. [P] His progress updates, archived at https://wob.coppermind.net/entry/3904 (summarised via search; the archive returned HTTP 403 when fetched directly)
- **Hemingway: stop mid-stream and reread.** Hemingway reread what he had written before continuing. He stopped each day while he still knew what came next, so the next session had an easy way in. He also kept a chart of his daily word count, he said, to avoid fooling himself. [S] Paris Review 1958, via Farnam Street, https://fs.blog/hemingways-routine/
- **Rachel Aaron (high-output genre): know the scene first.** Before each session, Aaron spends at least five minutes sketching the scene in shorthand on paper. She also logged start and stop time, word count and location for every session for two months, then looked for patterns. The patterns: no-internet locations doubled output, afternoons beat mornings, and long blocks were 3x more productive per hour than one-hour ones. When she couldn't find anything exciting in a planned scene, she cut or rewrote it. Her key rule: "know what you're writing before you write it". [P] Her 2011 post, Goodreads copy https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1273134-how-i-went-from-writing-2-000-words-a-day-to-10-000-words-a-day
- **Neil Gaiman: two inks, and the typed draft is draft two.** Gaiman drafts by hand in fountain pen and alternates two ink colours on different days. That way he can see each day's output at a glance. Typing the handwritten draft into the computer *is* his second draft, where he cuts. He stresses that nobody is meant to read a first draft. [S] Open Culture on the Tim Ferriss interview, https://www.openculture.com/2019/12/neil-gaiman-talks-dreamily-about-fountain-pens.html
- **Zadie Smith: scaffolding during the draft.** Smith uses imposed chapter structures or models borrowed from other books to get through a draft, then removes them, because the finished book usually stands without them. She also reads other novelists while drafting to balance her own prose. [P] https://thebeliever.net/?p=40223

**What slows authors down, in their own accounts:**
- **The first draft itself.** Robin Hobb calls it the hardest part. Each sentence narrows the possibilities, and a wrong choice means backing up and redoing the scene. [P] SFFWorld 2005, https://www.sffworld.com/2005/07/scifiint_163/
- **POV characters who still feel like strangers.** This is Abercrombie's account. [P] https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/17/joe-abercrombie-on-writing/
- **Not knowing what happens next.** This is Aaron's account. [P] https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1273134-how-i-went-from-writing-2-000-words-a-day-to-10-000-words-a-day
- **Structural knots where threads converge.** See Martin's Meereen problem, section 5.
- **Getting stuck deep in a manuscript.** King stalled about 500 single-spaced pages into *The Stand*. He says the boredom eventually produced the fix, which came suddenly. The specific fix (cutting down the cast) is reported inconsistently online. [T] Literature & Latte blog, https://www.literatureandlatte.com/?p=4869

## 3. Continuity, notes, bibles and timelines

### Long-series fantasy: authors who build an apparatus

- **Sanderson: a private wiki with page references.** Sanderson keeps a private wiki (WikidPad) on his own server, in his words "just like Wikipedia, but on my own server". After each book is written, a continuity reader reads it and adds notes *with page references*. There is a page per character, place, magic system, spren and so on. Sanderson consults it while drafting. [P] Sanderson FAQ, https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/?p=298
- **Sanderson's continuity editor and master timeline.** Karen Ahlstrom, his continuity editor, says she keeps records of every character, place, spren and even clothing. She checks eye colour and the like when a character reappears, and maintains a "Master Cosmere Timeline" spreadsheet. She calls Stormlight the hardest to keep straight, because of its storm cycles and many flashbacks. [P via search snippet; the page returned HTTP 403] https://wob.coppermind.net/entry/7832 ; [T] https://coppermind.net/wiki/Karen_Ahlstrom
- **Sanderson on continuity load.** He says continuity gets harder as viewpoints and complexity grow. [S] https://fantasy-faction.com/2014/brandon-sanderson-stormlight-interview
- **Robert Jordan: millions of words of notes.** Jordan's notes ran to millions of words, reportedly more than the published series, spread across what his assistant Alan Romanczuk called a "maze-like hierarchical system of files". Some files were very short and some long; some shared the same name and had to be merged later. Jordan started a proper-noun list, the "Raw Glossary", while editing book 1, and later handed it over to assistants. Early on he gave his assistant research questions such as whether two characters had ever met. After his death, Maria Simons and Romanczuk answered Sanderson's continuity questions, often by searching digital copies of the books. [P] Dragonmount interview with Maria Simons, https://dragonmount.com/blogs/entry/299-interview-with-maria-simons/ ; [P compiled] Theoryland, https://theoryland.com/intvmain.php?i=442 ; [S] WoT Companion listing, https://www.worldswithoutend.com/novel.asp?id=15700
- **Martin: outsourced fact-checking.** Martin recruited Elio García and Linda Antonsson of the fan site Westeros.org as fact-checkers for *A Feast for Crows*. They later co-wrote his world companions. [T] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elio_M._Garc%C3%ADa_Jr._and_Linda_Antonsson
- **Jemisin: separate notes files.** Jemisin has experimented with a personal wiki, but for *The Fifth Season* she kept extensive notes, including a list of the Seasons and a glossary of invented terms that became the book's glossary. She drew a map although she dislikes maps. [P] https://electricliterature.com/they-are-living-their-own-myths-an-interview-with-n-k-jemisin-author-of-the-fifth-season/
- **Abercrombie: character timelines, maps last.** Abercrombie keeps character timelines so ages and past events stay consistent. He draws maps late and bends them to fit the story. [P] https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/16/joe-abercrombie-on-planning/
- **Erikson and Esslemont: a shared world, loose timelines.** The Malazan co-creators played the world through in a role-playing game years before writing. They say it caused little continuity trouble because the arcs were "set in our minds". They also admit their timelines are not definitive. Their original world map got lost, and Erikson then added a new continent. [P] https://reactormag.com/a-conversation-with-malazan-series-authors-steven-erikson-and-ian-c-esslemont ; https://www.sffworld.com/?p=612 (both via search summary)

### Authors who keep almost nothing on paper

- **Gabaldon.** She says she keeps no notes: "if I write anything down, it's gone." Her historical timeline lives in her head, and research runs alongside the writing. [P] https://www.goodreads.com/questions/497460-hi--i-was-wondering-about-your-writing
- **Hobb.** The geography exists in her head while she writes, and maps come mostly afterwards. She wrote the series in chronological order. No source found describes a written bible. [T/P mix via search] https://www.scifinow.co.uk/interviews/interview-author-robin-hobb-a-k-a-megan-lindholm/
- **Nora Roberts.** No visual aids or colour-coded cards. She scribbles notes and often can't find them later. [S] https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1252769

### Grids and plans per chapter or instalment

- **J. K. Rowling: a hand-drawn grid.** Rowling plotted *Order of the Phoenix* on a hand-ruled grid. Rows are chapters. Columns hold chapter number, month, title and plot, plus one column for each subplot (e.g. "Prophecy", "D.A.", "Snape/Harry"). The subplots are tracked even in chapters where they never appear on the page. The sheet's origin is not formally confirmed. [S] Open Culture / Mental Floss, https://mentalfloss.com/article/26346/jk-rowlings-plot-spreadsheet ; https://electricliterature.com/j-k-rowlings-spreadsheet-shows-how-she-wrote-harry-potter/
- **Charles Dickens: one sheet per monthly instalment.** Dickens was the original chapter-by-chapter serial writer. Each monthly part got one folded sheet, which he called "Mems". The right half listed the instalment's chapters and their contents. The left half held open questions, long-range plans and motifs, which he later ticked off or answered in different ink. He noted when chapters ran long or short or were moved. He consulted the sheets before, during and after writing each part, so they worked as a running record of what was done and what still had to be carried forward. [Scholarly] https://dickensnotes.org/introduction/general ; Open Book Publishers edition, https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0092.pdf

**What this means.** Big-series authors converge on four kinds of record:
- an entity record: who, what and where, with attributes like eye colour
- a proper-noun glossary
- a master timeline, including character ages
- a chapter × thread grid

The best-resourced (Sanderson) tie each fact to a **page reference** in the published text, and mostly add facts *after* a book is finished, not during drafting. Many successful authors keep almost nothing written down. A bible must be optional and must never block drafting.

## 4. Revision: passes, cutting, reordering, salvage

- **King: cut about 10%, after six weeks away.** King's rule from an early rejection note is that the second draft should be the first minus about 10%. He recommends leaving the draft in a drawer for about six weeks, then reading it as if it were someone else's. [T] https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2088345-2nd-draft-1st-draft---10
- **Zadie Smith: months or years away.** Put a finished novel away for months or years so you can read it like a "smart stranger". Rereading early pages late in the draft shows what to trim. [P] https://thebeliever.net/?p=40223
- **Nora Roberts: three passes, each with a job.** Draft 1 is discovery. Draft 2 goes back to page one to fix, expand, cut and check that it all holds together; she enjoys this one most. Draft 3 is the polish. [S] https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1252769
- **Sanderson: numbered drafts with defined purposes.** For *Oathbringer*, 3.0 was a structural revision and 4.0 brought in comments from beta readers and his writing group. 5.0 was a polish to trim words; it cut about 12.5%. Proofreading ("gamma") was handed off after that. [P] https://wob.coppermind.net/entry/3916 (via search summary)
- **Sanderson: one big change at a time.** He finds it hard to hold more than one big change in his head while revising, and usually plans one major change per plot thread or POV. [P via search] https://wob.coppermind.net/entry/14268
- **Sanderson: overhauls are character problems.** On Writing Excuses he says he fixes an overhaul by brainstorming the fix, then rereading and revising every scene of that one character in order, often twice. [P] Writing Excuses 4.27, https://writingexcuses.com/?p=553
- **Abercrombie: what a first draft gets wrong.** His end-of-draft problems list: loose prose that needs cutting (especially early), shifting POVs, secondary characters whose roles changed, renamed places, and foreshadowing to adjust. One POV character went through two or three rethinks with whole chapters rewritten. He cut about 6,000 words between drafts while also adding material. [P] https://www.hachette.co.uk/fantasy/2012/07/18/joe-abercrombie-on-revising/
- **Jemisin: seed much, cut much.** She seeds a lot of material early and takes much of it out in revision. She also used an abandoned earlier draft of *The Hundred Thousand Kingdoms* as a detailed outline and rewrote the book from it. That is salvage at whole-book scale. [T] Summarised from the SFWA and Writing Excuses interviews via search: https://sfwa.org/2011/06/28/nebula-awards-2010-interview-n-k-jemisin/ ; https://writingexcuses.com/19-46-an-interview-on-structure-with-n-k-jemisin/

### Reordering and assembling pieces

- **Margaret Atwood: piles on the floor.** Atwood starts with a rough idea that "usually turns out to be wrong" and moves between longhand and computer. Once an arc appears, she prints chapters and moves piles around on the floor to test the order. [T] Alan Watt's blog, https://alanwatt.com/?p=11784
- **Martin: the "Meereenese knot".** In *A Dance with Dragons*, several characters' threads had to converge on one city in the right order, with consistent chronology. Working on it took years. Per fan-site reports of his blog, he split two large chapters into four so he could reorder events. He rewrote one chapter that had been "finished" four times, and switched to a new POV character to show the events. [T] https://awoiaf.westeros.org/index.php/Meereenese_knot
- **Gabaldon: chunks lined up on a timeline.** Gabaldon writes disconnected "kernels" out of order. They grow into 40–60-page chunks she is fairly sure will not change, and she lines the chunks up against her historical timeline and cause and effect. After about 18 months and four or five chunks she sees the book's "shape", and the rest goes fast. [P] https://www.writersdigest.com/january-12/get-more-gabaldon
- **Nabokov: index cards, ordered at the end.** From 1950, Nabokov drafted in pencil on index cards, out of sequence, and numbered the set only once it was complete. [S] Open Culture, https://www.openculture.com/2014/02/the-notecards-on-which-vladimir-nabokov-wrote-lolita.html
- **Hilary Mantel: from cards to binder.** In her essay "Growing a Tale", Mantel describes writing fragments on 3×5 cards or tear-out notebook pages and pinning them to a corkboard in no order. Related paragraphs get pinned behind the card. When a sequence appears, the pages move into a ring binder where they can be swapped. She called it growing a book rather than writing one. Keith Blount cites this essay as the model for Scrivener's corkboard and binder. [P quoted by L&L] https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/a-brief-history-of-scrivener-part-2
- **Writing Excuses hosts: reorder summaries, then rewrite.** Dan Wells reordered chapter *summaries* in a document and tracked the knock-on effects. When reordering failed, he added a character and rewrote chapters. Howard Tayler laid printed strips out on a table. [P] https://writingexcuses.com/?p=553

### Salvage and the "cuts" file

- **Laura Maylene Walter: "orphans".** Walter kept loved but cut passages as separate files in her rewrite folder. Saving them made cutting easier, because nothing was lost. Most orphans never went back into the book. [P] Kenyon Review blog, https://kenyonreview.org/?p=80251
- **Jamie Todd Rubin: a "Deleted Scenes" folder.** Rubin moves cuts into a Scrivener folder that is excluded from the compiled manuscript. [P] https://jamierubin.net/2009/03/13/deleted-scenes/
- **J. L. Curtis: scenes saved for later books.** A blog reports that Curtis writes scenes that fit a book several volumes ahead and slots them in when the timeline gets there. [T] https://madgeniusclub.com/2021/12/19/reduce-recycle-reuse/
- **Gaiman: cutting as saving.** Cutting in the typed second draft feels to him like saving work, not losing it. [S] https://www.openculture.com/2019/12/neil-gaiman-talks-dreamily-about-fountain-pens.html

### Feedback as structured data

- **Sanderson's beta reads.** Sanderson's beta readers received the manuscript in parts, about a week apart. They commented in a shared spreadsheet with **one tab per chapter**, plus "general reactions" tabs per part and for the whole book. They were told to look for continuity, timing and character or culture inconsistencies, and to ignore typos. Before posting, they word-searched the earlier books to check a detail. The proofreading ("gamma") sheet held only page number, error and fix. The format came from the Wheel of Time beta reads. [P, beta reader's account] https://reactormag.com/beta-reading-words-of-radiance/

## 5. Interruptions, ideas and inspiration mid-draft

- **Mantel: capture everything, sort later.** Mantel jotted fragments on cards or small tear-out notebooks: a name, a place, a single word. A 1998 profile says she still worked from notes written at bus stops and on trains. [P] https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/a-brief-history-of-scrivener-part-2 ; [S] https://www2.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/19981005/24212-hilary-mantel-the-novelist-in-action.html
- **Mantel: when stuck, leave the desk.** Her rule 9 says to step away when stuck: walk, bathe, cook, sleep. She advises against filling the gap with other people's words through calls or parties. [P, reprint of Guardian 2010] https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2056868-rules-for-writing-fiction
- **Dickens: a notebook of ideas, ticked off when used.** Dickens's *Book of Memoranda* (1855–1870) was an unordered notebook of possible titles, character names, snippets of dialogue, plot sketches and a pasted newspaper clipping. He drew on it for years: an 1855 title list and an 1862 plot idea both became *Our Mutual Friend*. He ticked or struck through used entries and noted where each one went ("Done in Our Mutual"). [Scholarly] https://www.english.cam.ac.uk/manuscriptslab/memoranda-and-mutual-friends-dickens-and-his-practices-of-note-taking-in-the-book-of-memoranda/
- **Octavia Butler: commonplace books.** Butler's commonplace books mix working notes for novels, research, journal entries and daily life. Her notecards hold working and research notes. Very little of it is indexed, which is a real cost for anyone retrieving it later. [Archival] https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8hm5br8/entire_text/
- **Gaiman: the "compost heap".** Gaiman describes letting things he absorbs rot down in a mental compost heap until they combine into something usable. Ideas come from noticing daydreams and asking "what if". [T] https://workingmethods.substack.com/p/neil-gaiman-go-where-your-obsessions
- **Jemisin: ideas gel over years.** Her ideas often begin in dreams or things she has done, and may gel together years later. [P] https://electricliterature.com/they-are-living-their-own-myths-an-interview-with-n-k-jemisin-author-of-the-fifth-season/
- **King: sceptical of idea notebooks.** King reportedly says a writer's notebook is the best way to immortalise bad ideas; good ideas stick around without one. This is paraphrased secondhand and the exact wording is unverified. [T] https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/23037686-writer-s-notebooks
- **Joan Didion: the notebook as a private record.** For Didion, a notebook is not a factual record but a private, erratic collection that keeps a writer in touch with who they were. [T] https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/11/19/joan-didion-on-keeping-a-notebook/
- **Nora Roberts: thinking happens away from the desk.** She works plot problems out in the shower, gardening, cooking and kneading dough, then can't find the scribbled notes. [S] https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1252769
- **Hobb: writing happens in the head first.** She drafts in her head and needs to step away from the screen to think; her fingers record it later. [P via search] https://ekultura.hu/2012/07/11/interview-robin-hobb-june-2012

**What this means.** Idea capture is universal, but it is messy, scattered across many places, and often lost (Roberts, Butler). Ideas are used much later and out of context (Dickens, Jemisin). The most useful habit found is Dickens's: mark an idea as used and say where it went.

## 6. Tools, and why authors choose them

- **Martin: WordStar 4.0 on an offline DOS machine.** He uses a separate computer for internet and email. He likes that WordStar does what he needs and nothing else, and he dislikes spellcheck and autocorrect, partly because they fight his invented names. [S] The Register, https://www.theregister.com/2014/05/15/game_of_thrones_written_on_brutal_medieval_word_processor_and_os/ ; Writers Write, https://www.writerswrite.com/george-r-r-martin-tells-51620141
- **Atwood: separate online and offline machines.** Atwood reportedly has two desks, one with an offline computer and one with an online computer. She starts books in longhand with a smooth-flowing pen. [S, via search] Daily Beast "How I Write" (URL unreachable when fetched)

## Implications for a writing app (Quarry)

### Support

1. **A plain drafting surface that stays out of the way.** No autocorrect. Spellcheck either off by default or fed from the bible's proper-noun list, so invented names are never underlined (Martin's complaint). Works fully offline, with no notifications while drafting (Martin, Atwood, Aaron).
2. **Forward motion without losing fixes.** While drafting, the writer can drop a "fix later" note anchored to a text span and keep going. Notes collect into a revision checklist that can be filtered by chapter, POV or thread. This serves Abercrombie's end-of-draft problems list and Sanderson's "one big change per thread or POV" revision.
3. **Re-entry aids.** On opening a chapter, show where the writer stopped and their last note to self, and make the previous session's text easy to reread (Hemingway, Lee Child). Offer an optional per-chapter scratch area for the writer's *own* pre-session shorthand (Aaron, Revis). Quarry never fills it.
4. **Named drafts and passes, not just autosave.** Snapshot a chapter or the book as "draft 1", "2.0 structural", "polish" (Sanderson's numbered drafts, Roberts's three passes, Lamott's down/up/dental drafts). Diffs between snapshots. A private "draft zero" status that never appears in exports (Pratchett, Gaiman).
5. **First-class salvage.**
   - A cuts bin ("orphans", "deleted scenes") where every cut fragment keeps its source: chapter, snapshot, date.
   - Fragments can be searched and tagged, and dragged back into any chapter. The link to the original location is kept.
   - Used fragments are marked and show where they went, like Dickens's "Done in Our Mutual".
   - Storage is cheap and nothing nags. Most orphans are never reused (Walter), but keeping them is what makes cutting painless.
6. **Pieces before order.** Even a linear chapter-by-chapter writer reorders and splits (Martin's knot, Atwood's piles). Support:
   - splitting a chapter into scenes and merging them back
   - a card or corkboard view of chapter and scene summaries that the *writer* writes
   - moving whole chapters
   - unplaced scenes that sit outside the reading order until they are placed (Gabaldon, Nabokov, Mantel)
7. **A bible whose facts point back to the text.** Each bible fact can cite the passage that established it, and the citation survives edits (Sanderson's wiki with page references; Ahlstrom's eye-colour checks). Support:
   - entity types for characters, places, systems, items and glossary terms
   - aliases and duplicate handling (Jordan's same-named files)
   - search across manuscript, bible and notes together, as Jordan's assistants searched digital copies to answer "have these two ever met?"
8. **Fill the bible after a chapter, not during it.** Sanderson's continuity reader adds facts *after* a book is done. Offer a light "chapter done: record new facts?" review step that the writer can skip. Never require bible entries to draft (Gabaldon, Hobb and Roberts keep little or nothing).
9. **Timeline and thread grid.** In-world dates on chapters and scenes; character birth dates so ages are computed (Abercrombie). A chapter × thread or POV grid modelled on Rowling's sheet, where a thread can be marked "happening off-page" in a chapter. A master timeline across books (Ahlstrom), which matters especially for flashbacks.
10. **A per-chapter ledger in the spirit of Dickens's "Mems".** One side shows what the chapter contains (the writer's own summary). The other shows open questions and threads to carry forward, each of which can be ticked or answered later. This is the closest historical match to a chapter-by-chapter serial pantser.
11. **An idea inbox that matches how ideas really arrive.**
    - Fast capture from phone or desktop, untyped and unsorted (Mantel's bus stops).
    - An optional source field ("from a book I was reading" or "from a conversation"), since this user's ideas come from reading and talking.
    - Linking or tagging to bible entities or chapters later.
    - Full-text search, so notes don't get lost (Roberts, Butler).
    - Mark as used, with where it went (Dickens).
12. **Structured feedback, separate from the text.** Per-chapter comment threads or import of reader notes, with a resolved / won't fix state. Modelled on Sanderson's one-tab-per-chapter beta sheet.
13. **Optional, private session statistics.** Words per session, time and place. Aaron found real patterns this way, and Hemingway charted words "so as not to kid myself". A per-day colour tint on newly written text could mimic Gaiman's two inks.
14. **Configurable process.** Authors say their process changes from book to book (Le Guin, Abercrombie, Jemisin, Mantel's rule 10). No fixed workflow, and passes, statuses and templates can be renamed.

### Avoid

- **Outline or beat-sheet templates as the default.** Many successful writers find outlines limiting or ignore them (King, Child, Martin, Jemisin's forgotten outlines).
- **Nagging and guilt mechanics.** No streaks, no "you haven't written today", no forced "update your bible" prompts. The bible and idea backlog should never feel like debt (King's scepticism about notebooks; Hobb and Roberts's irregular habits).
- **Anything that rewrites the writer's text.** No autocorrect, auto-capitalisation or "smart" renaming of invented words. Find-and-replace of a renamed place or character should always be explicit and previewed (Abercrombie's renamed places).
- **Destroying cuts.** Deleting a block of text should be able to send it to the cuts bin instead of nowhere.
- **Suggestions that cross into content.** Quarry must not suggest plot, names or prose. Bookkeeping prompts that only reflect the writer's own data are fine, for example "this capitalised word isn't in your glossary" or "this character's eye colour was recorded as X in chapter 4". Keep that boundary explicit and easy to switch off.
- **Treating "don't revise while drafting" as a rule.** Gabaldon, Zadie Smith, Dean Wesley Smith and Sanderson's section-by-section revision show that many writers revise as they go. Support both: inline editing *and* deferred notes.
