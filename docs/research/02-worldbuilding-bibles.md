# Worldbuilding, series bibles and continuity in practice

Research for Quarry's world model, 2026-10-22.

## Intro

Long fantasy series, franchises and TV shows all end up keeping the same thing: a reference of facts
about people, places, groups and things, plus a timeline. Somebody keeps it up to date, and it gets
checked against new text. The people doing this are a continuity editor (Sanderson), long-time
assistants (Jordan), outside fact-checkers (Martin), a lore keeper (Lucasfilm, *Game of Thrones*),
script supervisors and copy editors (film and publishing), and fan wikis that the authors themselves
end up consulting (Rowling, Martin).

Four findings matter most for Quarry:

1. **The timeline is the hardest part to keep, and the most valuable.** Each fact is tied to a
   chapter and a quote that backs it up.
2. **Most errors come from a small set of causes.** These are physical descriptions, ages, elapsed
   time, travel time, name spellings, and who knows or has met whom.
3. **Real projects separate four kinds of material.** These are facts established in the text,
   the author's private intentions, things characters believe, and contradictions the author
   meant to leave in.
4. **Fan wikis settle on a small common structure.** Each entry has an infobox, a list of
   relationships, a history told in book order, a first appearance, and citations to sources.

Source note: some sites block automated fetching (Coppermind, A Wiki of Ice and Fire, Fandom, Tolkien
Gateway). Where a claim comes only from search-result summaries, it is marked *(search summary)*.
All other sources were read directly.

## 1. How fantasy and SF authors organise worldbuilding and continuity

### Brandon Sanderson / Dragonsteel (continuity editor, wiki, master timeline)

- Karen Ahlstrom is Sanderson's continuity editor. She keeps an internal Dragonsteel wiki that began
  as Brandon's personal notes and covers characters, objects and places. It also holds detail that
  never appears in the books, such as highstorm speed or each highprince's house colours.
  *(search summary)* https://coppermind.net/wiki/Karen_Ahlstrom
- She also keeps the "Master Cosmere Timeline", a spreadsheet with **over 1100 lines** for
  Stormlight alone. Each row records:
  - the event;
  - the time elapsed since the previous event;
  - the book quote that justifies the timing;
  - the chapter;
  - whether the event happens on the chapter's own day or at another time.
  https://www.brandonsanderson.com/oathbringers-timeline
- From the same post:
  - **Parallel storylines.** Separate groups of characters have to be synced. Kaladin's and
    Dalinar's threads in *The Way of Kings* were about 50 days apart at the start and still a day
    or two off.
  - **Travel speeds.** She checks flying speed, army march rates, and whether a non-soldier could
    keep up 30 miles a day.
  - **Storm calculator.** A calculator gives the hour each storm reaches each city.
  - **Who fixes what.** Brandon gives a rough duration and she sets the exact one, for example
    turning "four days" of recovery into two so the storylines meet.
  - **Other records.** She also tracks characters, places, spren and clothing, including eye colour
    and food.
  - **Example errors.** A flashback showed a child who was not yet born, and a "month" of 45 days
    appeared where a Roshar month has 50.
  - **Relative dates.** She often has to reconcile timeframes that the text gives only as relative
    dates. https://www.brandonsanderson.com/oathbringers-timeline
- **Arcanum ("Words of Brandon")** is a fan-run archive of Sanderson's own statements, tagged by
  topic. Users can add tags and corrections. It works as a citable record of what the author has
  said outside the books. It is a fan project, not the Dragonsteel wiki. *(search summary)*
  https://wob.coppermind.net/about/
- **Sanderson's lectures on worldbuilding:**
  - He divides setting into **physical** (geography, climate, flora and fauna, cosmology),
    **cultural** (law and government, religion, technology, language, customs) and **magic**
    (rules, costs and limits, effect on society).
  - He keeps a "world guide" document with headings under setting, character and plot.
  - **Iceberg principle.** Most of the world stays below the page.
  - **Worldbuilder's disease.** He warns against building so much that writing stalls. His remedies
    are deadlines, iteration, and developing only a few elements in depth. He also advises: expand
    what you have before adding something new.
  https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025

### Robert Jordan / Team Jordan

- After Jordan's death, Maria Simons and Alan Romanczuk searched about **4 million words** of his
  notes to answer Sanderson's questions. Around 1997, Jordan had several note files with the same
  name, and he asked Simons to consolidate them. She kept a house glossary, and she answered
  questions like **"Has character A ever met Character B?"** She reads each manuscript for
  continuity errors. https://dragonmount.com/blogs/entry/299-interview-with-maria-simons/
- *The World of Robert Jordan's The Wheel of Time* (1997) was compiled from Jordan's notes.
  It is "broadly canonical", but it is **deliberately** vague, biased or wrong in places. It is
  written as an in-world document, so it shows how knowledge distorts over time and distance.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_of_Robert_Jordan%27s_The_Wheel_of_Time

### George R. R. Martin / Elio M. García Jr. and Linda Antonsson

- Around 2000, the founders of Westeros.org compiled a concordance of Westeros facts and sent it to
  Martin. Starting with *A Feast for Crows* they checked character and location details for him.
  Antonsson says their role covers setting and continuity, not story.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elio_M._Garc%C3%ADa_Jr._and_Linda_Antonsson
- A Wiki of Ice and Fire keeps **errata pages** of confirmed errors and inconsistencies. It notes
  that Martin believes accidental mistakes take attention away from the inconsistencies he planned.
  Martin has also said he regrets giving eye colours to so many characters, because checking them
  is a chore. *(search summary)* https://awoiaf.westeros.org/index.php/Errata_of_main_series
- The same wiki reports that the first release of the novella *The Sons of the Dragon* had errors,
  including the name spelled both "Rogar" and "Robar". García blamed a pre-correction manuscript
  being sent to the publisher. The claim is tagged citation needed. *(search summary)*
  https://awoiaf.westeros.org/index.php/The_Sons_of_the_Dragon

### J.R.R. Tolkien

- Tolkien based the LOTR moon phases on real years with a day offset. Sources disagree on which
  years: 1941–42 per Christopher Tolkien, or 1944–45 per a Mythlore article. He re-modelled the
  phases in 1944 to keep them consistent. Some small errors survived that only careful astronomical
  checking reveals. *(search summary)* https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Moon,
  https://dc.swosu.edu/mythlore/vol9/iss4/8
- *The Hobbit*'s moon phases cannot be reconciled with the LOTR chronology. One analysis shows they
  require an almost-new moon to become an almost-full moon overnight. *(search summary)*
  https://ece.uwaterloo.ca/%7Edwharder/Personal/Hobbit/A%20timeline%20for%20The%20Hobbit.pdf
- Appendix B ("The Tale of Years") and Appendix D (calendars) of LOTR are published examples of an
  author's chronology and calendar system. Christopher Tolkien's *The War of the Ring* records
  repeated revision of chronology and geography. *(search summary, general reference)*

### J.K. Rowling

- Rowling plotted *Order of the Phoenix* on a **hand-drawn grid**:
  - **Rows:** chapters in order, with the month running down the page.
  - **Fixed columns:** chapter number, month, title and plot.
  - **One column per subplot:** Prophecy, Cho/Ginny, D.A., Order of the Phoenix, Snape/Harry, and
    Hagrid and Grawp.

  Commentators say she did it because so much happens that she might not remember it all.
  https://www.openculture.com/2014/07/j-k-rowling-plotted-harry-potter-with-a-hand-drawn-spreadsheet.html
- Rowling has said she slipped into internet cafés to check facts on the fan-run **Harry Potter
  Lexicon** instead of buying her own book. The Lexicon covered characters, places, creatures,
  spells and objects. Its early timeline of events was later included with the film DVDs and
  accepted by Rowling. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harry_Potter_Lexicon

### Steven Erikson and Ian C. Esslemont (Malazan, shared world)

- The two authors built the world as a role-playing campaign, starting from **maps**. They admit they
  exchange information less often than they should. Erikson takes the blame for map errors and
  corrects a compass direction in one book's opening line. He says one author is left-handed and
  the other right-handed, which has repeatedly mixed up how they picture scenes. Both write strictly
  in sequence, and they clear major decisions, such as character fates, with each other.
  https://reactormag.com/a-conversation-with-malazan-series-authors-steven-erikson-and-ian-c-esslemont/

### Robin Hobb

- Hobb writes "very chronologically" and recommends reading in publication order to catch the
  cross-references. She does not plan themes in advance: the story grows from an idea, a setting
  and characters. https://www.lightspeedmagazine.com/nonfiction/interview-robin-hobb/
- Readers have pointed out description drift, for example Malta's hair changing colour between
  books. They asked whether it was an error or an in-world change. *(search summary)*
  https://www.goodreads.com/topic/show/1265114-ask-robin?page=4

### Patrick Rothfuss (fan-built timeline)

- A fan timeline of the Kingkiller books dates events **relative to an anchor**: Kvothe entering
  the University is year 0, and other events are −15, +9 and so on. Uncertain dates are marked
  with "~". Entries are labelled "(corroborated)" or "(disputed)", and an "Errata" colour flags
  known problems. One stretch of the timeline is noted as "essentially broken". The underlying
  in-world calendar (11-day spans and so on) had to be reconstructed from the text.
  https://reactormag.com/rothfuss-reread-timeline/

### Terry Pratchett / Stephen Briggs

- *The Discworld Companion* was co-written with Stephen Briggs. Later editions also drew on
  "The Pit", Pratchett's large file of Discworld notes. The first edition had "quite a few errors",
  corrected in later editions. Each entry carries source-book abbreviations showing where a detail
  appears. https://wiki.lspace.org/The_Discworld_Companion
- Wikipedia says the Companion came from Briggs's own database of Discworld information (tagged
  citation needed). Briggs also co-designed the first Discworld map, *The Streets of Ankh-Morpork*.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Briggs

## 2. Franchise continuity databases

- **Lucasfilm Holocron.** Leland Chee has maintained this FileMaker database since 2000. It
  replaced printed "bibles" of synopses and glossaries.
  - **Size:** over 55,000 entries, including about 19,000 characters, 2,900 species, 5,300 worlds
    and 2,100 vehicle types.
  - **Each entry records:** story and continuity information, **discrepancies between sources**,
    pronunciation, costume variations and behind-the-scenes notes.
  - **Alongside the entries:** timelines, maps, family trees and spelling dictionaries.
  https://www.starwars.com/news/what-is-the-holocron
- **How the Holocron is used.**
  - Material is entered early so that creators can "stake their claim" on new continuity.
  - New material is checked against what exists and what is still in development.
  - When sources conflict, Chee looks for an explanation that lets both stand.
  - Game teams also keep internal wikis.
  https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2009/10/08/feature-loremasters

## 3. TV and film: series bibles, writers' rooms, script supervisors

- **Series bible.** This is a reference on characters, settings and other elements. It is updated
  **once details are established on screen** or in scripts. The *Frasier* bible tracked small
  details such as Maris's food allergies and Frasier's mother's name. Writers' assistants and
  script coordinators act as "walking bibles". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_(screenwriting)
- **Contents of a bible.** Typically: characters with history and relationships, the show's
  "rules", sets and locations, and episode or season storylines. Writers keep it current so that
  season four does not contradict season two. *(search summary)*
  https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-steps-to-developing-your-tv-show-bible
- **Game of Thrones.** Bryan Cogman was the show's "Keeper of the Lore". He checked that what
  appeared on screen agreed with what came before and with the books.
  https://dothraki.com/?p=631 ; Martin's description, *(search summary)*
  https://georgerrmartin.com/notablog/?p=4355
- **Writers'-room whiteboards.** Separate boards cover season arcs (births, deaths, marriages and
  other milestones, plus the season timeline), episode outlines (A/B/C stories, with "runners" for
  recurring threads), character arcs (key relationships and turning points), a character list
  (main, recurring and guest), dialogue and catchphrases, and locations.
  https://networkisa.org/screenwriting_articles/view/13-types-of-tv-writers-rooms-whiteboards
- **"Carding".** Recurring characters are listed down the side of a board, with per-episode notes
  added on cards. This is a character × episode grid, much like Quarry's "where is everyone" grid.
  *(search summary)* https://infocusfilmschool.com/what-happens-in-a-writers-room/
- **Script supervisors.** They track actor positions, props, wardrobe, hair and makeup, eyelines and
  dialogue changes. Before shooting they produce a **one-line continuity synopsis** for each scene:
  time of day, **"day in story order"**, and a one-line summary. This keeps every department aligned
  on the passage of story time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_supervisor
- **Error types (continuity theory).** Common types are:
  - visual changes;
  - "editing" errors, where a character refers to something **they should not know yet**;
  - plot holes, such as an only child who later has a sibling;
  - age drift;
  - "Homeric nods", where a dead character reappears;
  - deliberate errors.

  Ways of handling them include retcons, fan explanations ("fanwank"), reboots and resets.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_(fiction)

## 4. Publishing: the copy editor's style sheet

- A Penguin Random House copy editor keeps running notes on chronology, logistics, plot and
  personal details: dates, ages, appearance, past experiences, and the layouts of houses and towns.
  She builds a detailed timeline and checks it against calendars and maps.
  - **Most common errors:** lapses in chronology and inconsistencies in characters' looks or
    backstory.
  - **Examples:** two full moons a week apart; blue-eyed parents with a brown-eyed child; a Sunday
    scene in a government office.
  - **Advice to authors:** "Outline, outline, outline."
  https://authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/maintaining-continuity-tales-from-the-copy-editor/
- **Fiction style-sheet sections.**
  - **Characters:** names, variant spellings and nicknames, physical description, age,
    relationships, occupation, speech patterns.
  - **Timeline and setting:** dates, seasons, time-of-day references, place names, distances.
  - **Invented terms:** with their capitalisation.
  - **Typical errors:** eye colour changing; a two-hour trip later taking thirty minutes; a town or
    name spelled differently in a sequel; invented technology working differently in book three.
  https://www.ebookpbook.com/2026/03/04/style-sheet-editing-books/

## 5. Fan wikis as accidental continuity databases

- **Wikidata's model of fiction** amounts to a formal version of what fan wikis do:
  - **Appearance properties:** *present in work*, *first appearance*, *last appearance*, and
    *narrative age*.
  - **Relationship properties:** spouse, sibling, father, mother, child, relative, student of,
    enemy, sidekick of, killed by, unmarried partner, and a generic *significant person* with a
    role qualifier.
  - **Time qualifiers on any statement:** **start work / end work**, which limit a fact to a range
    of works, and **time index**, a point within a work such as a chapter. This is very close to
    Quarry's "from/until chapter" model.
  - **Other qualifiers:** **spoiler-for** and **nature of statement**, which marks a statement as
    in-universe or out-of-universe.
  https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:WikiProject_Narration
- **Wikidata's fictional-universes project** adds *from narrative universe*, *home world* and
  *narrative location*. It notes that *narrative age* is qualified per work.
  https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:WikiProject_Fictional_universes
- **Star Wars wiki infoboxes** use homeworld, born and died (in-world dates such as "102 BBY"),
  species, gender, eye colour, affiliation, known masters and known apprentices. *(search summary)*
  https://swgames.fandom.com/wiki/Dooku
- **Other Fandom templates** add status (alive or dead), team or affiliation, and "Debut" or
  "Chapter Debut". In one template, Name, Picture and Debut are the only required fields.
  *(search summary)* https://portability.fandom.com/wiki/Infobox/Basics
- **Memory Beta** splits articles into **in-universe** entries (characters, places, objects,
  events) and **real-world** entries (sources, production). It also accepts that licensed sources
  contradict each other. *(search summary)*
  https://memory-beta.fandom.com/wiki/Memory_Beta:Inclusion_policy
- **What fan wikis converge on.** The structure is:
  1. an infobox of short facts;
  2. a relationships and family block;
  3. a history told **in book order**, often with sections like "Recent events: Book N";
  4. appearances, split into POV, appears and mentioned;
  5. per-statement citations to book and chapter;
  6. an errata page;
  7. separate timeline pages.

  The authors' own teams use these wikis: Rowling used the Lexicon, Martin's fact-checkers run
  Westeros.org, and Sanderson's continuity editor is compared with fan timelines. That shows the
  shape is good enough for professional use.

## 6. Common continuity failures and how they are caught

| Failure | Examples seen | How caught |
|---|---|---|
| Physical description drift (eyes, hair, handedness, height) | Martin's eye-colour regret; Malta's hair; style-sheet eye colour; Malazan handedness | Character sheet with "established at chapter X"; copy editor's style sheet |
| Ages and births versus events | Flashback shows an unborn child (Sanderson); age drift (Continuity) | Birth date plus timeline gives age at chapter; age-at-event checks |
| Elapsed time and calendar units | Roshar "month" of 45 vs 50 days; two full moons a week apart; Tolkien moon phases | Event timeline with relative offsets and a justifying quote; calendar definitions |
| Parallel storylines out of sync | Kaladin and Dalinar about 50 days apart; groups that must converge | Per-thread story-day counts compared where threads meet |
| Travel time | Westeros "teleporting"; 30 miles/day; 2 hours vs 30 minutes | Distance between places × travel mode speed versus elapsed time |
| Geography and direction | Malazan "west" for "east"; maps added late | Map or place records with relative positions |
| Name spelling and aliases | Rogar/Robar; town spelled differently in a sequel | Word list or glossary of proper nouns with variants |
| Knowledge state | A character refers to something they shouldn't know yet; "Has A ever met B?" | Tracking who knows what, and when; co-presence history |
| Rules of invented systems | Technology works differently in book three | "Rules" section of the bible; magic-system notes |
| Deliberate vs accidental contradiction | Martin's planned inconsistencies; Jordan's deliberately unreliable encyclopedia | Errata versus "intentional" marking |

Sources: see sections 1–4.

## Categories, fields and relationship types seen in practice

Consolidated from the Holocron, Sanderson and Dragonsteel, fiction style sheets, Wikidata, fan-wiki
infoboxes, TV bibles and Sanderson's lectures.

### Entry kinds
- **Characters (people), species or races.** Holocron, Wikidata, Fandom.
- **Places.** Worlds, regions, cities, buildings, plus house and town layouts (PRH copy editor).
- **Factions and organisations.** Houses, orders, armies, guilds; affiliation fields everywhere.
- **Objects and vehicles.** Artefacts, weapons, ships (Holocron vehicles); clothing (Dragonsteel).
- **Cultures and peoples.** Customs, religion, language, law (Sanderson's cultural setting).
- **Magic and technology systems.** Rules, costs, limits (Sanderson; style-sheet "invented
  technology").
- **Events.** Battles, births and deaths, meetings, storms (Holocron timelines, season-arc boards).
- **Lore and terms.** Glossary, invented words, spellings (Team Jordan glossary, style-sheet word
  list).
- **Calendars and astronomy.** Months, weeks, moons, storm cycles (Tolkien, Rothfuss, Roshar).
- **Plot threads and subplots.** Rowling's columns, TV "runners". These are not world entities, but
  writers track them on the same grids.

### Character fields
Name, aliases or titles, spelling variants, pronunciation, species, gender, born and died (in-world
date or relative), age (per work or chapter), status (alive, dead, unknown), homeworld or origin,
culture, affiliation(s), occupation or role, physical description (eye colour, hair, height,
handedness, distinguishing marks), clothing and costume variations, speech patterns or catchphrases,
personality, abilities, first and last appearance, appearances by chapter (POV, appears, mentioned),
image, behind-the-scenes or author notes, discrepancies.

### Place fields
Name and variants, type, parent place ("located in"), ruler or controlling faction, culture or
peoples, climate and geography, distances or travel routes to other places, map position, layout,
first appearance.

### Faction fields
Name, type, leader(s), members (time-bounded), seat or base place, allegiance or enemies, symbols or
colours (Alethi house colours), founding and dissolution.

### Object fields
Name, type, owner or holder (time-bounded), location (time-bounded), origin or maker, properties or
powers, appearances.

### Event fields
Name, date (absolute, relative to an anchor, or "N days after previous"), duration, place,
participants, chapter(s) where shown or mentioned, on-chapter-day versus off-day (flashback), the
quote that justifies it, certainty ("~", corroborated, disputed).

### Relationship types commonly tracked
- **Family:** parent and child, sibling, spouse, stepparent, godparent, relative (generic), heir.
- **Social:** lover or unmarried partner, friend, enemy or rival, ally, sidekick, mentor and student
  (master and apprentice), servant and master, liege and vassal, sworn to.
- **Person–faction:** member of, leader of, founder of, defector from.
- **Person–place:** born in, lives in or seat at, rules, located at (time-bounded, which feeds the
  grid), died at, visited.
- **Person–object:** owns, wields, made, lost, seeks.
- **Object–faction and object–place:** held by, kept at, symbol of.
- **Faction–faction:** allied with, at war with, vassal of, splinter of.
- **Person–event:** participated in, killed in, caused; killed by (Wikidata).
- **Knowledge:** knows (fact or secret), believes (false), has met. The last is derivable from
  co-presence.
- **Generic fallback:** "significant person" plus a free-text role (Wikidata P3342 with P3831).

### How time is modelled
- **Narrative position:** book, chapter or episode. Used by Wikidata *time index* and
  *start work / end work*, fan-wiki "Recent events by book", appearance lists, and Rowling's rows.
- **Story time:** in-world dates (BBY, Third Age, Shire Reckoning), "day in story order" (script
  supervisors), relative offsets from the previous event (Sanderson's column E), or offsets from an
  anchor event (Rothfuss year 0).
- **Both together:** Sanderson's sheet and the script supervisor's synopsis put chapter or scene
  next to story time. A separate flag marks flashbacks and events not on the chapter's day.
- **Uncertainty:** "~" for approximate dates, "corroborated" or "disputed" labels, and approximate
  durations from the author that the continuity editor then fixes.

## Implications for Quarry's world model

### Build

1. **Two time axes.** Keep chapter order (where the fact is established) separate from story time
   (when it happens in the world). "From/until chapter" covers most needs and is how Wikidata and
   fan wikis work. Add an **optional per-chapter story time**, entered in any of three ways: a
   free-text date, "N days after chapter X", or a story-day number. Add a "flashback / not on this
   chapter's day" flag. Sanderson's spreadsheet and script supervisors' "day in story order" show
   these are the minimum fields that work.
2. **Facts carry evidence.** Every fact, relationship or location span should be able to point at
   the manuscript passage that establishes it: chapter plus a quoted snippet or anchor. Sanderson's
   justifying-quote column, the Companion's source tags and fan-wiki citations all do this. For a
   pantser, the main way to add facts should be "select text in chapter → record as fact", with the
   chapter filled in.
3. **Separate what is established from what is only planned.** A TV bible is updated once things
   are on screen. Sanderson's wiki holds detail that never appears in the books. Quarry should mark
   each fact as **established in text** (with a citation) or as an **author note or intention**. The
   companion should check new text against established facts first, and treat author notes as
   softer hints.
4. **Belief and knowledge as data.** Knowledge errors ("refers to something they shouldn't know
   yet") and Team Jordan's "Has A ever met B?" are recurring problems. Support:
   - a time-bounded **"knows" relation** from a person to a fact or secret, from chapter X;
   - a **"has met" query derived from co-presence** in the location grid;
   - an optional "in-world belief, may be false" status. Jordan's deliberately unreliable
     encyclopedia shows that a character's belief is not the same as the truth.
5. **Discrepancies as first-class records.** The Holocron tracks discrepancies as a field, and fan
   wikis keep errata pages. When the companion flags a contradiction, the writer should be able to
   resolve it as **fix text / update fact / intentional (unreliable narrator, in-world change) /
   explain later**. "Intentional" must silence that flag permanently. Martin's planned
   inconsistencies and Hobb's hair-colour question both show why this matters.
6. **A small default field set per kind, with freeform extras.** Ship short defaults built from the
   consolidated list:
   - **Character:** name, aliases, spelling variants, born, died, status, physical (eyes, hair,
     height, handedness, marks), affiliations, origin, first appearance.
   - **Place:** type, located-in, controlling faction, travel links.
   - **Faction:** leader, seat, allies, enemies.
   - **Object:** holder, location, origin.
   - **Event:** when, where, participants, chapter.

   Allow user-defined fields as well. Physical traits and ages should be **time-bounded facts**
   ("eyes: grey, established ch. 3"), not plain strings. That lets the companion say where each
   value came from.
7. **Aliases and name variants first.** Rogar/Robar and town names misspelled in sequels are among
   the cheapest errors to catch. Keep alias and variant lists per entry and use them to (a) detect
   mentions in the manuscript, which also feeds the location grid and "unrecorded connection"
   hints, and (b) flag near-miss spellings of known names.
8. **Rules for the companion, roughly from cheapest to most valuable:**
   - **Unknown names:** a capitalised name in a chapter that matches no entry or alias, or an
     alias spelled almost the same as a known name.
   - **Unrecorded connections:** two entries co-occur in a chapter or scene but no relationship is
     recorded. Suggest only, never auto-create.
   - **Value conflicts:** the same attribute has two different values whose validity ranges
     overlap (eye colour, faction membership, holder of an object).
   - **Life-span checks:** a dead character appears (not as a flashback or mention), or a character
     appears before their recorded birth (Sanderson's unborn-child case).
   - **Two places at once:** a person is in two places in the same chapter, or in the same story
     time across parallel threads (Kaladin/Dalinar sync).
   - **Knowledge order:** a person refers to a fact before their "knows" relation starts.
   - **Travel time (optional):** a place-to-place move needs less story time than distance divided
     by mode speed. Run this only when the writer has entered distances and the chapters have
     story time.
9. **The "where is everyone" grid comes from the data, not from a separate board.** Rows are
   characters, columns are chapters, and cells come from time-bounded "located at" facts plus
   detected mentions, marked POV, present or mentioned. This is the same layout as Rowling's grid
   and TV carding boards. A second view with plot threads as rows would serve the same pantser
   need without adding a new entity type.
10. **Relationship types: a starter set plus custom types.** Ship the common list above, grouped by
    the pair of kinds involved. Each type has a direction and an optional inverse label (parent ↔
    child, master ↔ apprentice). Keep Wikidata's escape hatch: a generic "connected to" with a
    free-text role, so the writer is never forced to pick a taxonomy mid-draft.
11. **In-universe text and author notes stay separate.** Follow Memory Beta's in-universe /
    real-world split and Wikidata's "nature of statement". Each entry should keep in-world
    description apart from author notes ("planned reveal", "based on…", "TODO decide").

### Do not build (or build late)

- **No calendar or astronomy engine up front.** Tolkien's moon phases and Roshar's storm calculator
  are real needs, but only in the most extreme cases. Start with free-text dates plus relative day
  offsets, and add a custom calendar (month lengths, week names) later if writers ask. Never model
  astronomy.
- **No GIS or map-scale geography.** Martin first discouraged measuring his maps, and Malazan's maps
  drifted. Optional distance or travel-time values on place-to-place links are enough for
  travel-time checks.
- **No mandatory templates or long infoboxes.** Sanderson's "worldbuilder's disease" and the
  pantser workflow both argue against forms. Every field is optional and an entry can be just a
  name. Fan wikis make only Name and Debut required. In Quarry, a "first appearance" can be
  computed from the text.
- **No canon tiers or multi-source canon management.** The Holocron's discrepancy machinery serves
  many authors and media. One author needs only "established", "note" or "intentional
  inconsistency".
- **No automatic fact extraction written into the bible.** Rules may *suggest* (co-occurrence,
  unknown name, conflicting value), but only the writer writes facts. This matches "no AI writes
  story content" and how human continuity editors work: they query, the author decides.
- **No blocking of the writer.** Flags are a quiet, dismissible list, not errors that stop writing.
  Contradictions are sometimes the point, as with Jordan's unreliable encyclopedia and Martin's
  planned inconsistencies.
- **No public wiki or publishing features.** The value is a private, queryable bible for one writer.

### Open questions for the design

- **What the grid is keyed on.** Chapters are the obvious unit, but long fantasy chapters often
  hold several scenes in different places. Sanderson's sheet and script supervisors both work per
  scene or event, so the grid needs at least an optional scene level.
- **How a chapter's story time is entered.** It could be per chapter, or per POV thread so that
  parallel storylines can be synced (Sanderson's hardest problem).
- **Who owns the citations when text is revised.** For a pantser who revises heavily, citations
  must survive edits (anchors that move with the text, or fuzzy re-matching of quotes). Otherwise
  the evidence links go stale.
